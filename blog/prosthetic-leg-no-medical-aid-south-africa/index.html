How to get a prosthetic leg in South Africa without medical aid

Farida Cajee-Botes, Orthotist & Prosthetist2 September 20268 min read

Can you get a prosthetic leg for free in South Africa?

Yes, through the public health system. State hospitals fit prostheses through provincial orthotic and prosthetic centres, and patients classified as fully subsidised (the H0 category) receive assistive devices and prostheses free of charge. You do not need a medical aid, and you should never pay anyone a fee to be put on a list. What you do need is a referral, proof of your income status, and a great deal of patience.

One thing is worth saying plainly at the start. This is a private practice, and private prosthetics is expensive. If you have no medical scheme, no road accident claim and no work injury claim, the state route below is your route, and pointing you at it properly is more useful to you than selling you an appointment. The rest of this article sets out each funding route, what it does and does not entitle you to, and how to work out quickly which one applies to you.

How do you get referred for a prosthesis at a state hospital?

Start at your nearest government clinic, or at the hospital where the amputation was done. Clinic services in South Africa are free for everyone, and it is the clinic or hospital team, usually a doctor, physiotherapist or occupational therapist, who writes the referral through to a provincial orthotic and prosthetic centre (Section27, Your Right to Health Care). You cannot normally be seen at one of those centres without that referral in hand.

The centres are few. In Gauteng, the provincial health department names four hospitals as its dedicated prosthetic and orthotic centres: Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital, Dr George Mukhari Academic Hospital, Steve Biko Academic Hospital and Tambo Memorial Hospital. In the six months from April to September 2023 the province reported issuing 376 prostheses, alongside 13,461 orthoses and other assistive devices (Gauteng Department of Health). Ask your referring clinician which centre serves your area, and ask for the referral in writing.

Ask for one more thing at the same appointment: a referral for pre-prosthetic rehabilitation. Research published in the African Journal of Disability in 2025 found that patients are often referred to an orthotic and prosthetic centre for a prosthesis without being referred for the rehabilitation that has to happen first, which sends them back to a district hospital and adds months to an already long process (Visagie et al., African Journal of Disability). Find the hospital social worker early too. They handle fee classification, transport problems and, at some facilities, know which donor programmes are currently running.

What will a prosthesis cost you at a state hospital?

For most South Africans, nothing. Public hospitals classify every patient under the Uniform Patient Fee Schedule. If you receive a social grant (a disability grant, older persons grant, child support grant, care dependency grant, foster child grant, war veterans grant, social relief of distress grant or grant-in-aid), or you are formally unemployed with written proof, you fall into the H0 category and receive services free of charge (Western Cape Provincial Gazette 8902 of 2024).

The published Western Cape schedule is explicit about devices. Under the line item covering assistive devices and prostheses, H0 patients pay nothing, H1 patients pay 10% of the full cost and H2 patients pay 20% (Western Cape Provincial Gazette 8902 of 2024). The income bands are consistent across provinces: for the tariffs Gauteng introduced on 1 April 2025, H1 covers individuals earning under R70,000 a year and households under R100,000, H2 runs up to R250,000 for an individual and R350,000 for a household, and above those figures you are H3 and pay the full fee (Gauteng Department of Health). The same 2025 research estimates that roughly 70% to 80% of the population qualifies as fully subsidised, and is therefore eligible to receive a prosthesis free of charge through the public system (Visagie et al.).

Take proof with you: your ID, and either your SASSA letter confirming the grant and the period it covers, or written proof of unemployment from the Department of Employment and Labour. The Western Cape regulations state that a sworn affidavit is not accepted as proof of unemployment, so get the real letter. Fee schedules are gazetted province by province and revised annually, so confirm the current position with the facility treating you, and if you are unsure of your category, ask to be assessed rather than assuming you will be charged.

How long is the wait in the public sector, and what do you get?

Long, and it would be dishonest to soften that. Research published in the African Journal of Disability in 2025, focused on the Eastern Cape, records users waiting from 10 months to six years for a device, and some waiting three to four years simply to be measured. That province's prosthetic backlog grew from more than 600 devices in 2018 to 1,276 by 2024 (Visagie et al., African Journal of Disability).

The reasons are structural rather than personal. In the same province fewer than half of the established orthotic and prosthetic posts were filled, with two of six chief positions and nine of 38 assistant posts occupied, and delayed payments to suppliers stopped production for months at a time. The paper also cites an earlier estimate that only around 8% of South African amputees are ever fitted with a prosthesis. Those figures describe one province, but understaffing and procurement delay are reported widely.

The options available in the government sector are fewer than in the private sector (Össur South Africa), so expect a functional, basic limb rather than a carbon-fibre or microprocessor-controlled build. That is a real limitation, and it is also a working leg that costs you nothing. Practically: get onto the list now rather than when you feel ready, keep a copy of every referral letter, write down the name of the person you speak to at each visit, and phone to confirm your place rather than waiting for a letter to arrive.

Do Prescribed Minimum Benefits help if you have no medical aid?

No. Prescribed Minimum Benefits are an obligation on medical schemes, so they only help you if you belong to one. If you do, they matter. Amputation falls inside the roughly 271 diagnosis and treatment pairs every scheme must fund on every plan option, and the Council for Medical Schemes confirms that amputations following trauma, severe burns, diabetes, peripheral artery disease, severe infection and cancer must be funded under the PMB regulations (Council for Medical Schemes).

PMB decisions are meant to be diagnosis-based, and ordinary scheme exclusions do not apply to them (Council for Medical Schemes). In practice, interpretation varies between schemes, and a prosthetic claim can still be paid from the wrong benefit or partly declined. Where the annual appliance benefit and the PMB route together still leave a shortfall, some schemes will consider an ex gratia payment: a discretionary, once-off contribution decided by a committee outside your normal benefit structure. It is always worth applying for, but no scheme is legally required to grant it (SAOPA). We go through the scheme side in more detail in does medical aid cover prosthetics and orthotics.

What if the amputation happened at work?

Then the Compensation Fund pays, not you and not your medical aid. Its service book states that the Fund pays the reasonable cost of artificial devices according to published medical tariffs, that every artificial device must be pre-authorised first, and that the outcome of a request should be available within 15 working days (Department of Employment and Labour, COIDA Service Book).

You may use any HPCSA-registered orthotist or prosthetist who is registered with the Fund. Two things to sort out first, though. The accident must be reported to your employer, who submits the employer's report of an accident; without a claim number nothing moves. And the COIDA tariff list allows for basic prosthetic components only, so higher-specification componentry has to be combined with another funding source or paid for privately (SAOPA). Cover sits with different insurers by industry: mining and metals workers are generally covered through Rand Mutual Assurance and construction workers through Federated Employers Mutual rather than the Compensation Fund directly, so ask your employer which one carries your cover (Össur South Africa).

What if the leg was lost in a road accident?

Then the Road Accident Fund is your route, and it is the only one that can fund replacement limbs for life. Anyone injured in a crash involving a motor vehicle may claim, pedestrians and passengers included, except a driver who was the sole cause. A successful claim can carry an undertaking under which the Fund pays for future treatment and goods as they are incurred (Road Accident Fund).

The deadlines are unforgiving, and missing one is the most common way this route is lost. A claim must be lodged within three years of the accident where the driver is identified, and within two years in a hit-and-run where the driver is not, and it must be finalised within five years of the accident (Road Accident Fund). You do not have to appoint an attorney in order to lodge: the Fund employs information officers at its branch offices to assist claimants free of charge, although you may still use a lawyer if you prefer (Road Accident Fund). Once an undertaking is in place, the RAF will generally fund a replacement prosthesis about once every five years rather than on request (SAOPA).

Are there charities that give away prosthetic legs in South Africa?

A few, and they are narrower than most people hope. Two were verifiable at the time of writing. Jumping Kids Prosthetic Fund, started in 2009 by a prosthetist and registered as a non-profit company, supplies lower-limb prosthetic equipment and rehabilitative care to children from previously disadvantaged backgrounds (Jumping Kids). Zimele NPC, founded in 2020 and based in Cape Town, fits prostheses and rehabilitates adult amputees from disadvantaged backgrounds (Zimele).

Beyond those two, we could not verify a national programme that supplies free prosthetic legs to adults on request, so we are not going to name one. If a page or a person asks you for an upfront fee to register you for a free prosthesis, that is not how any route described here works. Your hospital social worker remains the best local source, because the donor programmes that do exist tend to be small, regional and not online at all.

If one of the funded routes does apply to you (a medical scheme, an RAF undertaking or a COIDA claim), that is where a private practice earns its place: assessing you properly, specifying componentry that matches how you actually live, and preparing the clinical motivation and itemised quotation your funder requires. You can read more about our prosthetics service, the conditions we manage, or what drives the figures in how much does a prosthetic leg cost in South Africa. If you would like to talk it through, get in touch, and if the public route is the right answer for you, we will tell you so. Key takeaways

  • In the public sector a prosthesis is free for fully subsidised (H0) patients: social grant recipients and the formally unemployed with written proof. In the published Western Cape schedule, H1 patients pay 10% of the cost of an assistive device or prosthesis and H2 patients pay 20%.
  • Access starts at your nearest government clinic or the hospital that did the amputation, which refers you on to a provincial orthotic and prosthetic centre. Gauteng names four: Chris Hani Baragwanath, Dr George Mukhari, Steve Biko and Tambo Memorial.
  • The wait is the hard part. Eastern Cape research published in 2025 records users waiting from 10 months to six years for a device, with a provincial backlog of 1,276 devices by 2024.
  • Prescribed Minimum Benefits only apply if you belong to a medical scheme. Amputation is covered by the PMB regulations, and an ex gratia application can be made for a shortfall, but no scheme is obliged to grant one.
  • COIDA covers work-related amputations and pays the reasonable cost of artificial devices at published tariffs, basic components only, with pre-authorisation required first.
  • The Road Accident Fund can fund replacement limbs for life through an undertaking, but a claim prescribes after three years (two years for a hit-and-run), and you can lodge one without an attorney.
